# Break architecture

Plan a break before writing it: one thought, an opening unlike the recent ones, claims in the order a curious listener would ask for them, and an ending that lands. Everything factual stays inside the cited evidence.

## Procedure
1. List what the cited evidence can support, one fact per line with its source id, and count the claim lines you can ground. If the evidence supports fewer claim lines than the station needs, do not pad with general knowledge: say what is missing and ask for more evidence or a shorter slot.
2. State the one thing a listener should be able to say after the break, in one sentence. A break that makes three points makes none.
3. Choose the opening against the recent ones. Read the patterns-to-avoid brief and the last ten openings, then pick first words and a form (a question, an address to the listener, a plain statement, a concrete detail) that none of them used. A hook that works can be one option; it is never the default.
4. Order the break: opening, then claims, then at most one analogy and at most one signposted return to the topic (never on the first line), then an ending that is a cited point or a single sign-off.
5. Tease and pay off. If the opening raises a question, the ending answers it. A tease that is never paid off is worse than none.
6. Give every short reaction a job. It picks up a word or idea from the line before it. A stock reaction appears once at most, and not the one the previous script used.
7. Write the lines, run the station checks tool, and fix what it flags. Do not judge the result: the Programme Director critiques it and a human listens.

## Verification
The plan names the one thought, the claims with their source ids, the opening chosen and how it differs from the recent ones, and anything left out for lack of evidence. No fact, number or name appears that the evidence does not contain. The checks output is quoted, and no verdict on quality is given.
