# Unscripted feel

Make two hosts sound as if they are listening to each other, using only what the cited evidence contains. In pre-written audio that is structure, not decoration: each reply comes from the line before it. Nothing here may add a fact, a claimed experience or a stage direction.

## Procedure
1. Write one reply at a time. Before each reply after the first, name the word or point in the previous line that it answers, and write from that, not from the outline. The question an aircheck sheet asks of a pair is whether they are "listening to each other, rather than simply following a script" (NPR).
2. Every reply adds something the last line did not: an objection, a consequence the evidence states, a correction, a concession, or a question that follows from it. Cover the last line. If the reply says the same thing again in new words, cut or rewrite it. Never hand the other host's key phrase back for emphasis; weak co-hosts "repeat or paraphrase points" (Radio Ink).
3. Let questions come from a tension. Look for two cited facts that seem to pull against each other (a memory that is always on, and a command that turns memory off). Let the foil notice the tension first and the other host settle it from the source. A host asks because the asker would not know, never to hand the other a cue to read out a fact: no "quick quiz", no "guess what", no question whose answer is the next line. A host may ask which the other would reach for first and why, as a what-if tied to a cited detail.
4. Reactions answer something. A short reaction ("hang on", "wait") picks up the exact word, number or contradiction in the line before and is followed by a reason or a question. No free-floating "right", "yeah" or "for sure": random, repetitive interjections are what made AI hosts sound uncanny (DW). No platitude such as "that's the million dollar question".
5. Let the talk turn once. One host takes the point somewhere the other did not expect (a consequence, a doubt, a different conclusion) and the other answers that, not the outline. Say yes to the point and move it forward with the next cited fact: "The Yes, and technique is critical to an engaging co-host" (Radio Ink).
6. Imperfection is optional and bounded. At most one short false start or self-correction in a script ("hang on, let me say that cleaner"), in colour only. Never inside a fact, number, name, command or flag. If there is no safe place, use none.
7. One recap at most. A single line may gather the point, spoken by one host, and the other host reacts to it in a line of its own rather than summarising again. Do not end both hosts on the same sentence.
8. Never fake a past. Hosts have no history: no "last time", "this morning", "I always", "I have never". Use what-ifs with no time or past in them ("if you ran it without the flag") and sharp observation of the material in front of them.
9. Run the station checks tool and fix what it flags. Then read each reply against step 1: can you point to the word it picked up? Hand the scripts over without grading them.

## Verification
Every reply after the first picks up a named word or point from the line before and does not repeat it. Each question comes from a tension or a real gap, and none is a quiz. No bare filler interjection and no platitude. At most one self-correction, outside every cited fact. At most one recap line. No host claims a past or a time such as "this morning". No stage directions. No verdict on how natural the script sounds.
