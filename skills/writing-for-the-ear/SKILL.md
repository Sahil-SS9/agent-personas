# Writing for the ear

Write what a person would say, not what a page would show. A listener cannot re-read.

## Procedure
1. One thought per sentence. If a sentence needs "and" twice, split it. Vary line lengths on purpose: a run of lines the same length sounds like a list.
2. Keep each turn short enough to say in a breath or two. The station stops one host speaking for more than 90 words in a row; stay well under that.
3. Say numbers; do not read them. Give a figure the way a listener would hear it ("a little over three times") only when the cited evidence contains it. Never invent or sharpen a figure. A number belongs in a claim line with its citation, not in a chatty aside.
4. Describe code, paths, flags and settings instead of spelling them. Say what the setting does ("the base URL setting") rather than reading `model.base_url`. Avoid backticks, underscores, slashes, double dashes and arrows: the voice may read them aloud or stumble, and nothing in the station removes them.
5. No markup, emoji or stage directions. The station's prompt asks for none and nothing strips them. Delivery marks belong to the On-Air Performance Director, not to the script.
6. Repeat, do not synonym: use the same word for the same thing within a break. Write acronyms the way they are said.
7. Apply the would-you-say-it test to every line. You cannot hear the voice, so say that plainly and leave the sound to a human listener.

## Verification
No symbol the voice may read badly remains. Every number is cited and speakable. The longest turn is well under 90 words and line lengths vary. The report says what was checked by reading and by tool, and that nothing was heard.
